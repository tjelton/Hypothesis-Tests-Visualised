The 'NULL' Hypothesis

The null hypothesis for a test of independence is that the two variables are independent — that knowing the value of one variable tells you nothing about the value of the other.

If the variables really were independent, then within the population the proportion falling in any combination of categories would just be the product of the two separate (marginal) proportions. This is the idea we will use to work out the expected frequencies shortly.

The Alternate Hypothesis

As with the goodness of fit test, there is no choice to make for the alternate hypothesis. The alternate hypothesis is simply that the two variables are associated (i.e. not independent) — that the value of one variable does tell you something about the value of the other.

Expected Frequencies

The expected frequency of a cell is the count we would expect to see if the null hypothesis were true (i.e. if the two variables were independent). For the cell in row \( i \) and column \( j \) it is calculated as:

$$E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total}) \times (\text{column } j \text{ total})}{\text{grand total}}$$

The intuition: under independence, the proportion in row \( i \) is \( \frac{\text{row } i \text{ total}}{\text{grand total}} \) and the proportion in column \( j \) is \( \frac{\text{column } j \text{ total}}{\text{grand total}} \). Multiplying these proportions together and scaling back up by the grand total gives the formula above.

Assumptions

For the chi-square test of independence to be valid, we need to check the following assumptions:

The first assumption is that the observations are independent of one another. In practice, this means that each individual (or item) contributes to exactly one cell of the contingency table — nobody is counted twice, and no observation's category is influenced by another's.

Careful — do not confuse the two "independences"! This assumption (independence of the observations) is NOT the same as the independence between the two variables that the hypothesis test is investigating. The test can only tell us about the relationship between the variables; it relies on the observations already being independent by design. If the same person appears in more than one cell, the test is invalid no matter what it reports.

How do we check? We check by investigating the experimental setup — how the data was collected — making sure each individual was recorded once and gathered at random.

The chi-square distribution is only a good approximation when the expected counts are not too small. Cochran's rule gives a common guideline, which holds when both of the following are true:

  • none of the expected counts are 0, and
  • no more than 20% of the expected counts are less than 5.

Test Statistic

The test statistic measures how far the observed counts are from the expected counts, summed over every cell of the table:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{all cells}} \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$

We build it up in two steps. Use the tabs below to move through them.

For each cell we take the observed count minus the expected count. The table shows observed − expected = difference in each cell.

Next, for each cell we square the difference and divide by the expected count, giving \( \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \). Adding these up over all cells gives the test statistic.

The 'Chi-Square Test of Independence'

Suppose a researcher wonders whether being a coffee drinker is associated with being a night owl. These are two qualitative variables: whether a person drinks coffee (yes / no) and whether they are a night owl (yes / no). The researcher surveys 140 people and records both answers for each person, producing a grid of counts called a contingency table:

Night OwlNot a Night OwlRow total
Drinks Coffee503080
No Coffee204060
Column total7070140

The null hypothesis is that the two variables are independent — that whether someone drinks coffee tells us nothing about whether they are a night owl. The alternate hypothesis is that they are associated:

\( H_0: \) coffee drinking and being a night owl are independent.

\( H_1: \) coffee drinking and being a night owl are associated.


If the variables were independent, we can work out the count we would expect in each cell using the row and column totals. For the cell in row \( i \), column \( j \):

$$E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total}) \times (\text{column } j \text{ total})}{\text{grand total}}$$

For example, the "Drinks Coffee & Night Owl" cell has expected count \( \frac{80 \times 70}{140} = 40 \). Doing this for every cell gives expected counts of 40, 40, 30 and 30. The test statistic then sums \( \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \) over all cells:

$$\begin{align*} \chi^2 &= \frac{(50-40)^2}{40} + \frac{(30-40)^2}{40} + \frac{(20-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(40-30)^2}{30} \\ &= 2.5 + 2.5 + 3.3333 + 3.3333 \\ &= 11.6667 \end{align*}$$


To turn this into a p-value we use the chi-square distribution. For a test of independence the degrees of freedom equal \( (\text{rows} - 1) \times (\text{columns} - 1) \). Here that is \( (2-1)(2-1) = 1 \). As always for a chi-square test, a large statistic means the observed counts are far from what independence predicts, so we look at the upper tail:

\( p = P(\chi^2_1 > 11.6667) \approx 0.00064 \)


This p-value is tiny, so the observed counts would be very surprising if the variables were independent — we would reject the null hypothesis and conclude that coffee drinking and being a night owl are associated.
