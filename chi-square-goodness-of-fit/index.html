The Alternate Hypothesis

Unlike some other tests, in a chi-square goodness of fit test there is no choice to make for the alternate hypothesis. The alternate hypothesis is simply that the null hypothesis is wrong — that is, at least one of the category proportions is not equal to its hypothesised value.

We write this as: at least one of the equalities in \( H_0 \) does not hold.

Assumptions

For the chi-square goodness of fit test to be valid, we need to check the following assumptions:

The first assumption is that the observations are independent of one another. This means that whether one observation falls into a particular category has no effect on the category any other observation falls into.

For example, if we roll a die 60 times, each roll is independent: the face that comes up on one roll tells us nothing about the face on the next roll. This assumption would be violated if, say, we counted the same set of observations twice, or if observations were selected in a way that let one influence another (for instance, asking a group of friends who tend to agree, rather than a random sample of people).

How do we check? We check by investigating the experimental setup — how the data was collected — rather than by looking at the numbers themselves. We ask whether each observation was gathered separately and at random, so that no observation could influence another.

The chi-square distribution is only a good approximation when the expected counts are not too small. Cochran's rule gives a common guideline, which holds when both of the following are true:

  • none of the expected counts are 0, and
  • no more than 20% of the expected counts are less than 5.

Test Statistic

The test statistic measures how far the observed counts are from the counts we would expect under the null hypothesis. It is calculated by working out, for each category, the quantity \( \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \) (where \( O \) is the observed count and \( E \) is the expected count), and then summing across all categories:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

The 'Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test'

Suppose you have a six-sided die, and you are suspicious that it might not be fair. A fair die should land on each of its six faces with equal probability, that is, a probability of \( \tfrac{1}{6} \) for each face. To investigate, you roll the die 60 times and record how many times each face comes up.

The single qualitative variable here is "the face the die lands on", and it has 6 categories (the faces 1 through 6). We set up a null hypothesis that the die is fair — every face is equally likely:

\( H_{0} : p_1 = p_2 = p_3 = p_4 = p_5 = p_6 = \tfrac{1}{6} \)


The alternate hypothesis is simply that the die is not fair — at least one face does not have probability \( \tfrac{1}{6} \):

\( H_{1} : \) at least one of the above equalities does not hold.


We model the null hypothesis with the box on the right. Because a fair die has equal proportions, the box simply holds one ticket for each face: \( 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 \). Drawing a ticket at random is like a single roll of a fair die.

Now suppose that across the 60 rolls we observed the following counts:

If the die were perfectly fair, we would expect each face to appear \( 60 \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 10 \) times. The test statistic measures how far our observed counts are from these expected counts, by summing \( \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \) over every category:

$$\begin{align*} \chi^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^{6} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \\ &= \frac{(8-10)^2}{10} + \frac{(12-10)^2}{10} + \frac{(9-10)^2}{10} + \frac{(11-10)^2}{10} + \frac{(15-10)^2}{10} + \frac{(5-10)^2}{10} \\ &= 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 2.5 + 2.5 \\ &= 6 \end{align*}$$


To turn this into a p-value, we use the chi-square distribution. Its shape is controlled by a single number, the degrees of freedom, which for a goodness of fit test equals the number of categories minus one \( (k - 1) \). Here that is \( 6 - 1 = 5 \). Because a large test statistic means the observed counts are far from the expected counts, we always look at the upper tail — the area to the right of the test statistic:

\( p = P(\chi^2_5 > 6) \approx 0.306 \)


This p-value is quite large, so the observed counts are not surprising for a fair die — we would not reject the null hypothesis. With this example in mind, it is now your turn to use the app to run your own chi-square goodness of fit test. Good luck :)
